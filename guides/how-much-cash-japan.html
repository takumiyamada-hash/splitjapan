How Much Cash Do You Actually Need in Japan? A Calculator, Not a Guess

Updated August 2026 · Figures marked as estimates are a planning model — your trip is the real input, which is why there's a calculator below instead of one magic number.

Ask this question in any travel forum and you'll get answers from "barely any, it's 2026" to "¥20,000 a day, trust me." Both are true — for the person saying them. The honest answer is that your cash need is a function of four things: how you eat, where you go, how long you stay, and how many of you there are. So let's build the number instead of guessing it.

The baseline fact: Japan is cashless-ish

Japan's official cashless payment ratio reached about 58% in 2025 (METI, March 2026). Cards and tap payments now work at essentially every convenience store, chain restaurant, department store, train station and hotel. If your whole trip were konbini, chains and hotels, you could genuinely carry almost nothing.

The catch: the remaining cash-first economy isn't randomly distributed. It's concentrated in exactly the experiences people come to Japan for:

  • Small izakaya and family-run restaurants — many remain cash-only, and even card-accepting ones may refuse cards for small amounts
  • Ramen shops with ticket machines — the classic food-ticket vending machine is overwhelmingly a cash device (newer ones increasingly take IC cards)
  • Shrines and temples — offerings, omamori charms, goshuin stamps: cash, usually coins
  • Festival stalls, street food, markets — cash, sometimes exact-change culture
  • Rural buses and country train lines — some don't take IC cards at all; drivers can't break big notes
  • IC card top-ups themselves — charging a physical Suica at machines is effectively a cash transaction for foreign-card holders (details)

Reading your result

For a typical city-focused trip (Tokyo–Kyoto–Osaka, one local meal a day), the model lands around ¥3,000–4,000 per person per day in actual cash — which matches what most experienced travelers report carrying. Street-food-heavy itineraries and countryside legs push it toward ¥6,000–8,000. If a number like "¥50,000 for the trip" feels high, remember it's not extra spending — it's the same spending, routed through paper.

Getting the cash: fewer, bigger withdrawals

  • Where: 7-Eleven (Seven Bank) and Japan Post ATMs are the two networks that reliably take foreign cards, with English menus. They're everywhere.
  • Fees: expect a small ATM usage fee per withdrawal (typically in the ¥110–¥220 range depending on network and amount) plus whatever your home bank charges. Per-withdrawal limits apply (commonly up to ¥100,000; lower for some cards).
  • Strategy: because fees are per-withdrawal, two big withdrawals beat ten small ones. Withdraw your calculator total in 2–3 goes, not daily top-ups.
  • Decline the conversion: if an ATM or card machine offers to charge you in your home currency, always choose yen. That "convenience" (dynamic currency conversion) typically costs several percent.

The group-trip wrinkle

Cash is where group expense tracking goes to die. Cards leave statements; the ¥18,460 izakaya bill one friend covered in cash leaves nothing. Two weeks later nobody remembers who paid at the shrine, who covered the market lunch, who fed everyone's Suica. The fix is cheap: whoever pays, log it in the moment — fifteen seconds on SplitJapan, in yen, no app for anyone else to install — and settle everything once at the end, in each person's home currency. (And before you fly out, deal with the coin mountain.)
