Otoshi: The Japanese Seat Charge Nobody Warned You About

Updated August 2026

You sit down at an izakaya. Before you've ordered anything, a tiny dish arrives — some simmered vegetables, maybe a bit of potato salad or marinated fish. Nice touch, you think. Then the bill comes and there it is: a few hundred yen per person for food you never asked for.

Search any Japan travel forum and you'll find furious first-timers convinced they've been scammed. You haven't. Here's what actually happened.

What otoshi is

Otoshi (お通し) — called tsukidashi (突き出し) in western Japan — is a small appetizer served automatically at izakaya and many casual drinking establishments, and billed per person, typically ¥300–¥600. Functionally, it's a cover charge with food attached. The dish is the receipt: it signals to the staff that your order has been "opened" and your seat is now paying rent.

Why does Japan do it this way? Izakaya economics. You can occupy a table for three hours nursing drinks and small plates; there's no tipping and no minimum spend. The otoshi is the floor — a guaranteed few hundred yen per seat that keeps the lights on. Seen that way, it's the honest version of the service fees and mandatory tips other countries fold into the bill.

Can you refuse it?

Legally it's a gray-ish zone that has been debated in Japan for years; practically, here's what a traveler needs to know:

  • Most izakaya will not remove it, even if you don't touch the dish. It's a seating charge; sending the food back doesn't return the seat.
  • A minority of places — especially ones used to foreign customers — may waive it if asked politely before anything is served. It costs nothing to ask once, and everything to argue after.
  • If a charge appears that seems far above the normal range (say, ¥1,000+ per person at an ordinary-looking bar with no explanation), that's not otoshi culture — that's a venue to walk out of politely and not return to. Standard izakaya are transparent; tourist-trap bars exist in nightlife districts everywhere.

None of these replace tipping — Japan still doesn't tip — they're just where the economics live instead.

Budgeting for it

For a group, otoshi quietly adds up: four people × ¥500 × a two-week trip with nightly izakaya visits is over ¥25,000 of "invisible" charges. Two practical notes:

  1. When comparing izakaya bills to home, mentally add ~¥500 a head before you're surprised. It's the cost of the seat, not an error.
  2. Since otoshi lands on the table's single bill — and Japanese restaurants often won't split that bill — one person pays and the group settles later. That's a fifteen-second entry in SplitJapan: total in yen, split evenly, otoshi included, done.

The reframe that fixes the feeling

The anger about otoshi is really about surprise, not money — ¥500 is less than most people tip at home. Once you know the rule, the little dish becomes what it actually is: the izakaya's handshake. You've paid for the seat; now stay as long as you like.
