How Much Will Your Japan Tax Refund Actually Be? (Most Calculators Get This Wrong)

Tax-free shopping

How much tax refund will I actually get in Japan?

After a typical 1.55% handling fee on the pre-tax price, about 8.45% on general goods and about 6.45% on food and non-alcoholic drinks — not the flat 10% most calculators show.

Why is the refund different for food?

Japan charges consumption tax at two rates: 10% on general goods and a reduced 8% on food and non-alcoholic beverages. Tax-free shopping refunds the tax actually charged, so food refunds less. Alcohol is always 10%.

Is the ¥5,000 minimum before or after tax?

Before tax. At 10% that is about ¥5,500 on the price tag, and at 8% about ¥5,400. Purchases must be in one store on one day, so consolidating helps if you are near the line.

Japan Tax-Free Shopping Is Changing on November 1, 2026 — Here's Exactly What To Do

Tax-free shopping

What changes for tax-free shopping in Japan on November 1, 2026?

The in-store discount ends. You pay the full price including consumption tax at the register and claim the tax back when you leave Japan, after customs confirmation. Sealed bags and the ¥500,000 consumables cap are abolished.

Do I get the full 10% back?

No. Refunds are processed by the retailer or a refund operator, who deduct a handling fee — department stores have historically taken about 1.55% of the pre-tax price, with figures up to 2.2% reported for the new system.

What is the minimum spend for tax-free shopping in Japan?

¥5,000 pre-tax, in one store, on the same day. This is unchanged under the new system.

"Pay in Yen or Dollars?" — Always Yen. Here's the Math on Japan's Sneakiest Fee

Cash & cards

Should I pay in yen or my own currency in Japan?

Always choose yen. The home-currency option is dynamic currency conversion, where the merchant's payment provider sets the rate with a margin built in — estimates of the cost range from about 2.6% to as much as 12%.

Where will I be offered dynamic currency conversion?

At ATM screens after entering the amount, at hotel checkout, at department store terminals popular with tourists, and in currency dropdowns on booking sites that default to your home currency.

How Much Cash Do You Actually Need in Japan? A Calculator, Not a Guess

Cash & cards

How much cash should I bring to Japan?

For a city-focused trip, roughly ¥3,000–4,000 per person per day in physical cash. Street-food-heavy itineraries and countryside legs push that toward ¥6,000–8,000.

Is Japan cashless now?

Partly. The official cashless payment ratio reached about 58% in 2025. The remaining cash-first economy is concentrated in small izakaya, ticket-machine restaurants, shrines, festival stalls and rural buses.

Where can I withdraw cash with a foreign card in Japan?

7-Eleven (Seven Bank) and Japan Post Bank ATMs reliably accept foreign cards and have English menus. Expect a per-withdrawal fee typically in the ¥110–220 range plus your own bank's charge, so make fewer, larger withdrawals.

Leftover Yen: What To Do With Japanese Coins Before You Fly Home

Cash & coins

What should I do with leftover Japanese coins?

Spend them as you go at convenience store self-checkouts, which have a coin tray that counts the whole handful. On the last day, Pocket Change kiosks at major airports convert leftover yen to digital money, or use an airport donation box.

Can I exchange Japanese coins back home?

Generally no. Currency exchange counters outside Japan accept notes but refuse coins, so whatever metal is in your pocket when you fly is effectively a souvenir.

Money in Japan:The Complete Guide

Should I exchange money before flying to Japan?

Only a token amount, if it helps you feel calm. Withdrawing yen in Japan from a 7-Eleven or Japan Post ATM is normally the better rate than a currency counter at your home airport, and those two networks are the reliable ones for foreign cards. The real preparation is not exchanging cash - it is carrying two cards from two different banks, and knowing that the machine at a small station may never have accepted foreign cards at all.

Do I need to tell my bank I am travelling to Japan?

It is worth five minutes. Japanese card failures have three causes and your bank owns one of them: an issuer fraud block on an unfamiliar country. A travel notice, or at least the bank's app installed on your phone so you can answer the “was this you?” prompt, removes that cause. It does not help with the other two - Japan's 3-D Secure requirement online, and machines that never took foreign cards.

Which Japan travel apps are not worth installing?

Six categories you can skip: currency converters (memorise one anchor and always pay in yen), tipping calculators (Japan does not tip), JR Pass calculators (since the 2023 price rise the nationwide pass rarely pays off for a Tokyo-Kyoto-Osaka trip), dedicated phrasebook apps (Translate plus five words covers it), Wi-Fi finder apps (a dead category once you have an eSIM), and a second or third transit app just in case.

What exchange rate should you use when settling up after a trip to Japan?

The rate at the moment each expense happened, not the rate on the last day. Converting everything at one closing rate quietly moves money between people: whoever paid early, when the yen was cheaper or dearer, absorbs the difference. Lock the rate per expense and the yen total and everyone's home-currency share both stay honest.

Otoshi: The Japanese Seat Charge Nobody Warned You About

Dining & etiquette

What is otoshi?

A small appetizer served automatically at izakaya and billed per person, typically ¥300–600. It functions as a seat charge with food attached. In western Japan it is called tsukidashi.

Can I refuse otoshi?

Most izakaya will not remove it, even if you do not touch the dish, because it is a seating charge rather than an order. Some places used to foreign customers may waive it if you ask politely before anything is served.

Is otoshi a scam?

No. It is a standard charge that lets a venue with no tipping and no minimum spend hold a table for you. A charge well above the normal range with no explanation at a nightlife bar is a different matter.

Suica Money Traps: Expiring Balances, Foreign Phones, and Cash-Only Charging

Cash & cards

Does Suica work on my phone?

iPhones have Suica built into Apple Wallet and can top up with Apple Pay. Most Android phones bought outside Japan lack the FeliCa chip and cannot use Mobile Suica at all, so a physical card is the answer.

Can I get a refund on my Suica balance?

Not for tourist-oriented Welcome Suica. The physical card expires after 28 days and the mobile version after 180 days, and the remaining balance is not refunded. Spend it down before you fly.

Why can't I top up my Suica with a credit card?

Topping up a physical card at station machines is effectively a cash transaction; foreign credit cards do not work there. Phone-based Suica can top up from Apple Pay instead.

The Group Trip Money Playbook for Japan: Who Pays, How to Track It, How to Stay Friends

Group travel

How should a group handle money on a Japan trip?

One payer per venue, log the expense before you stand up, never settle mid-trip, and settle once at the end. Restaurants often will not split bills and much of the spending is cash that leaves no statement.

Why shouldn't we repay each other during the trip?

Tipping in Japan: Don't. Here's What Happens Instead

Dining & etiquette

Do you tip in Japan?

No. Not at restaurants, taxis, hotels, hairdressers or ramen counters. Leaving money on a table usually results in staff chasing you down the street to return it.

If there is no tipping, what is the extra charge on my bill?

Service economics appear as fixed line items instead: otoshi of ¥300–600 per person at izakaya, a 10–15% service charge at hotels and high-end restaurants, a posted table charge at some bars, and a roughly 20% late-night taxi meter between about 10pm and 5am.

Are there any exceptions where tipping is accepted?

Rarely and always optionally: private guides who work mainly with Western clients sometimes accept tips, and kokorozuke at a high-end ryokan is a fading tradition. If you do it, money goes in an envelope, never as bare notes.

Why Japanese Restaurants Won't Split Your Bill (and What To Do Instead)

Dining & etiquette

Can you ask for separate checks in Japan?

Sometimes. The phrase is betsu-betsu, and it usually works at chain restaurants and casual lunch spots. At busy izakaya it is often refused, and course menus or all-you-can-drink bookings are always one bill.

Why won't Japanese restaurants split the bill?

Point-of-sale systems track the table rather than the seat, so itemising shared plates means staff reconstructing your evening from memory during the dinner rush.

What should a group do instead?

One person pays the whole table and the group settles privately afterwards. It is faster, it works at cash-only places, and it is what Japanese groups do.

Why Your Credit Card Gets Declined in Japan (and the Fixes That Actually Work)

Cash & cards

Why does my credit card get declined in Japan?

Three common causes: the card is not enrolled in 3-D Secure, which Japan requires for online card payments; your bank's fraud engine blocked a first-ever yen transaction; or the verification SMS cannot reach a data-only travel eSIM.

My card works in shops but fails on Japanese websites. Why?

In-person payments do not use 3-D Secure, so a card that is not enrolled will pass at a till and fail online. Buying at a station counter is the reliable fallback.

Should I pay in yen or my home currency?

Always yen. Accepting the home-currency option is dynamic currency conversion, which hands the exchange rate to the merchant's payment provider and typically costs several percent.
